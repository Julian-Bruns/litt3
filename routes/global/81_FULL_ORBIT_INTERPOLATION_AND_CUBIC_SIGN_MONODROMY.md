# Full orbit interpolation and cubic sign monodromy

**Status: proved; independently audited (PASS, 2026-09-04).**

Auditor: `c14_elliptic_translation`.
[Verdict and non-breaking observations](audits/81_FULL_ORBIT_INTERPOLATION_AND_CUBIC_SIGN_MONODROMY_AUDIT.md).
The audit found no breaking objections and reran the exact certificate.

This note replaces the unproved quadratic-core assertion in files 66 and
68 by a construction that retains all hyperelliptic square-root choices.
No older special case is deleted. The issue with the older inference is
that compositum degrees alone do not determine intersection degrees.

The replacement works in every degree. Its extra parameter is the degree
of a finite scheme of sign choices, bounded by (2^r), independently of
the common-cover degree. For cubic orbits this parameter belongs to

\[
                         j\in\{1,2,4,8\}.
\]

The case (j=4), absent from the proposed quadratic reduction, admits an
additional uniform genus bound. This also repairs the missing case in the
degree-six argument for the explicit curves in file 76.

## 1. The full orbit field

Let (k) be algebraically closed of characteristic different from two,
and let (r\ne\operatorname{char}k) be an odd prime. Assume

\[
 g(X)=s+1,\qquad g(Y)=rs+1,\qquad k(Y)=k(t,z),\quad z^2=f(t),
\]

and an equal-degree diamond of finite etale maps

\[
 V\xrightarrow{a}Y,\qquad V\xrightarrow{p}C\xrightarrow{c}X,
 \qquad \deg p=r,\quad \deg a=\deg c=M,
\]

where (p) is a cyclic torsor with generator (\beta), and

\[
                              a\beta\ne a.
\]

Put (F=k(C)), (K=k(V)), and

\[
 t_i=\beta^i(t),\quad z_i=\beta^i(z),\qquad 0\leq i<r.
\]

The (t_i) are distinct: otherwise the prime-order action fixes (t),
and its odd order then forces it to fix (z), contrary to the last
condition. Let (B\subset F) be the field generated by the coefficients
of the monic polynomial

\[
                       P(T)=\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}(T-t_i).
\]

As usual, write (B) also for its smooth projective curve and put

\[
 e=[F:B],\qquad E=B(t),\qquad d=[E:k(t)]=2M/e.
\]

The coefficient curve has a birational projective model of degree (d).

There is a unique polynomial (Q(T)\in K[T]) of degree less than (r)
such that

\[
                              Q(t_i)=z_i\quad(0\leq i<r).
\tag{81.1}
\]

The cyclic action permutes these interpolation conditions, so every
coefficient of (Q) belongs to (F). Define

\[
 D=B(\text{coefficients of }Q)\subset F,\qquad E_*=D(t).
\tag{81.2}
\]

### Theorem 81.1 (the full orbit square)

Set (j=[D:B]) and (m=[F:D]). Then

\[
 e=mj,\qquad [E_*:D]=r,\qquad [K:E_*]=m,\qquad FE_*=K.
\tag{81.3}
\]

Thus (V) is the normalization of the integral fiber product

\[
                            C\times_D E_*.
\tag{81.4}
\]

The maps (V\to E_*\to Y) are finite etale. If

\[
                            N=\deg(E_*\to Y),
\]

then

\[
             N=M/m=jd/2,\qquad m\mid M,
             \qquad 1\leq j\leq 2^r.
\tag{81.5}
\]

Moreover, (j=1) if and only if (z\in E). In all other cases (j)
is even. All the extensions in (81.3) are separable.

#### Proof

The polynomial (P) is irreducible over (F), of degree (r), and
therefore irreducible over (D\subset F). Since (Q(t)=z), the field

\[
                        E_*=D(t)=D(t,z)
\]

contains (k(Y)). Also (F(t)=K). Consequently

\[
                     [E_*:D]=[FE_*:F]=r.
\]

This equality, rather than an inference from an intersection alone,
proves that (F\otimes_D E_*) is the field (K). It gives (81.3)
and (81.4). The intermediate maps of (V\to Y) are etale, so their
degrees give (N=M/m). Comparing (e=mj) with (ed=2M) gives

\[
                              jd=2N.
\]

To bound (j), work over the field (B) with its finite etale algebra

\[
                              A=B[T]/(P).
\]

The element (f(T)\in A) is nonzero and hence invertible, because

\[
                         A\simeq B(t)=E
\]

is a field. The scheme of solutions (q\in A) of (q^2=f(T)) is
finite etale of degree (2^r): after a separable splitting extension
it is the product of the (r) two-element square-root schemes. The
coefficients of (Q) describe one point of this scheme, with residue
field (D). Hence (D/B) is separable and (j\leq2^r).

If (j=1), then (Q\in B[T]), so (z\in E). Conversely, if

\[
                             z=Q_0(t),\qquad Q_0\in B[T],
 \qquad\deg Q_0<r,
\]

applying (\beta^i) gives (z_i=Q_0(t_i)). Uniqueness of
interpolation gives (Q=Q_0) and (D=B). Otherwise (E(z)/E) is
quadratic and is contained in (E_*/E), whose degree is (j). Thus

\[
                              2\mid j.
\]

Finally, (E_*/D) is separable because (P) has distinct roots, and

\[
                         K/E_*\quad\text{is etale}.
\]

The degree equality already proved identifies its base with (F/D),
which is therefore separable as well. \(\square\)

### Theorem 81.2 (norm and genus bounds for the corrected square)

Suppose (J(Y)) is absolutely simple. Then

\[
                     rs+1\leq g(D)\leq Ns+1,
                     \qquad N\geq r.
\tag{81.6}
\]

For (\eta=Ns+1-g(D)\geq0), the exact different degrees are

\[
 \deg\operatorname{Diff}(C/D)=2m\eta,
 \qquad
 \deg\operatorname{Diff}(E_*/D)=2r\eta.
\tag{81.7}
\]

If (J(X)) is also absolutely simple, then

\[
                  m\geq\operatorname{gon}(X)
                  \quad\text{or}\quad N\geq r+2.
\tag{81.8}
\]

#### Proof

Put

\[
 h_D=(E_*/D)_*(E_*/Y)^*:J(Y)\longrightarrow J(D).
\]

If this map vanished, the moving degree-(r) divisors on (Y)
associated with (E_*\to D) would lie in a single base-point-free
linear system, giving a degree-(r) map (Y\to\mathbf P^1), as in
Lemma 68.2. Such a map and the hyperelliptic map of degree two have
coprime degrees and give, by Castelnuovo--Severi,

\[
                              g(Y)\leq r-1,
\]

a contradiction. Thus (h_D\ne0), and simplicity of (J(Y)) gives

\[
                              g(D)\geq rs+1.
\]

Since (E_*\to Y) is etale, (g(E_*)-1=Nrs).
Riemann--Hurwitz for (E_*\to D) gives the upper bound in (81.6).
Applying it also to (C\to D), using (M=mN), proves (81.7).

The normalized square (81.4) gives, as an identity of divisor
correspondences,

\[
                 p_*a^*=(C/D)^*h_D.
\]

Its pushforward to (J(X)) vanishes, since (J(Y)) is simple of
larger dimension than (J(X)). If ((C/D)_*c^*=0), the same
moving-divisor argument gives a degree-(m) pencil on (X). Otherwise
its image has dimension (s+1), and is orthogonal to the image of

\[
                              h_D.
\]

Both fit in (J(D)), so (g(D)\geq(r+1)s+2). Comparing with

\[
                              g(D)\leq Ns+1
\]

forces (N\geq r+2). This proves (81.8). \(\square\)

## 2. The finite group retaining the square roots

Let

\[
                    L=B(t_i,z_i:0\leq i<r)\subset K.
\]

This is the splitting field of (P), followed by all the indicated
square roots. It is Galois over (B). Its group (H) acts faithfully
on the (2r) symbols ((t_i,\pm z_i)), preserving their (r) pairs,
so

\[
                            H\leq(C_2)^r\rtimes S_r.
\tag{81.9}
\]

Let (H_0) stabilize the sign selection

\[
                             \{(t_i,z_i):0\leq i<r\}.
\]

Interpolation proves (D=L^{H_0}). The restriction of (\beta)
belongs to (H_0) and cycles these (r) elements. In particular

\[
                    j=[H:H_0],\qquad |H_0|\mid r!.
\tag{81.10}
\]

For (r=3), the projection of (H) on (S_3) and its subgroup

\[
                              H_0\subset S_3
\]

both contain a three-cycle. Their orders are therefore three or six.
The kernel of the projection is a two-group. Thus (j) is a power
of two; (81.5) gives (j\in\{1,2,4,8\}).

## 3. A uniform bound for the cubic four-choice case

Assume here that (r=3), \(\operatorname{char}k=5\), and (j=4).

### Theorem 81.3 (tetrahedral sign choices)

In this case (H\simeq A_4\) or (S_4\). Every nontrivial inertia
group of (L/B) has order three. Moreover

\[
                  g(E)=\frac{3ds}{2}+1,
\tag{81.11}
\]

and the spectral incidence curve gives

\[
                   g(B)\geq\frac{ds}{2}-\frac{2d}{3}+1.
\tag{81.12}
\]

Consequently, if the coefficient system has dimension at least three,

\[
                                  d\geq s+2.
\tag{81.13}
\]

If in addition (3\nmid m), then (L/B) is etale and the stronger
formulas hold:

\[
                    g(B)=\frac{ds}{2}+1,
                    \qquad d\geq s+3.
\tag{81.14}
\]

#### Proof

Identify the eight sign choices with the vertices of the binary cube,
with the chosen selection at (000). The three-cycle (\beta)
has two fixed vertices (000,111) and two orbits of size three:
the vertices of weights one and two. An (H)-orbit of size four
containing (000) must consist of (000) and one of these orbits.
The choice of the weight-one orbit is impossible: the Hamming distances
from (000) to the other three vertices are all one, whereas from
any other vertex they are (1,2,2). Signed permutations preserve
Hamming distance, so they cannot act transitively on that set.

Thus the orbit is the four even-parity vertices. Its stabilizer in
the signed permutation group is

\[
                       (C_2)^2\rtimes S_3\simeq S_4.
\]

Its action on these four vertices is faithful. The transitive subgroup

\[
                              H\subset S_4
\]

contains a three-cycle, so it is (A_4) or (S_4).

Put (E'=B(t_0,z_0)\). Since (E'\subset L\subset K) contains

\[
                              k(Y),
\]

the maps (L\to E'\to Y) are intermediate etale maps. The subgroup
fixing a signed root is \(\operatorname{Gal}(L/E')\). At a point of

\[
                              L,
\]

its intersection with inertia is trivial, and the same holds for
every conjugate. Thus inertia acts freely on all six signed roots.
The group order divides 24, so ramification is tame and inertia is
cyclic.

No involution in the even-parity signed permutation group acts freely
on the six signed roots. If its permutation of the three pairs is
trivial, it changes exactly two signs and fixes the third pair. If
it exchanges two pairs, its two exchanged sign changes agree; even
parity forces it to fix both signs of the remaining pair. Hence any
cyclic inertia group of even order is impossible. In (A_4) and

\[
                              S_4,
\]

the only remaining nontrivial cyclic order is three.

The quadratic map (E'\to E) is therefore etale: all ramification
indices inherited from (L/B) are odd. By Theorem 81.1's original
spectral degrees, (E'\to Y) has degree (d), so

\[
                              g(E')=3ds+1.
\]

Etale Riemann--Hurwitz for (E'\to E) proves (81.11).

The spectral equation (P) cuts out an integral curve in

\[
                              B\times\mathbf P^1
\]

of class (A\boxtimes\mathcal O(3)), where \(\deg A=d\).
Its normalization is (E), and adjunction gives arithmetic genus

\[
                              3g(B)+2d-2.
\]

Comparison with (81.11) gives (81.12). A birational nondegenerate
projective curve of degree (d) has genus at most

\[
                              \frac{(d-1)(d-2)}2.
\]

Combining this with (81.12), and cancelling (d>0), yields

\[
                              d\geq s+\frac53.
\]

Integrality gives (81.13).

Finally assume (3\nmid m). The degree \([K:L]\) divides (m),
since \(E_*\subset L\). If \(\beta\) fixed a point of (L), it
would act freely on the fiber of the etale map (V\to L\), as it
acts freely on (V). That fiber would have size divisible by three,
contrary to \(3\nmid[K:L]\). Thus this three-cycle fixes no point
of (L). All order-three subgroups of (A_4\) or (S_4\) are
conjugate, so no order-three inertia remains. The cover (L/B) is
etale.

Its intermediate cover \(E_*\to B\) is then etale of degree

\[
                        [E_*:B]=rj=12.
\]

Since \(g(E_*)-1=3Ns\) and \(N=2d\), this gives

\[
                              g(B)-1=ds/2.
\]

The projective genus bound now gives \(d\geq s+3\).
\(\square\)

## 4. Repairing the degree-six reduction

### Corollary 81.4

For the explicit pair in file 76, the coefficient possibilities at

\[
                              M=6
\]

reduce to coefficient degrees one and two, both already excluded by
the surface and cubic-closure arguments of file 78. Thus the degree-six
exclusion survives without the invalid general quadratic-core inference.

#### Proof

Here \(r=3,s=8\), both Jacobians are simple, and

\[
                              \operatorname{gon}(X)=3.
\]

The coefficient system has dimension at least three by Proposition
68.4a, whose proof does not use the quadratic-core assertion. Hence

\[
                    ed=12,\qquad d\geq2,
                    \qquad e\in\{1,2,3,4,6\}.
\]

If \(j=1\), then \(m=e\) and \(N=6/e\). The lower bound

\[
                              N\geq3
\]

leaves \(e=1,2\), and (81.8) excludes \(e=2,N=3\).

If \(j=2\), then \(m=e/2\) and \(N=12/e\). For \(e=6\)
the bound \(N\geq3\) fails; for \(e=4\), (81.8) fails because

\[
                             m=2<3,\qquad N=3<5.
\]

Only \(e=2\) remains.

If \(j=4\), divisibility \(j\mid e\) leaves \(e=4\), with

\[
                              m=1,\qquad d=3.
\]

Theorem 81.3 requires at least \(d\geq s+2=10\), a contradiction.
The value \(j=8\) cannot divide any remaining \(e\).

Thus only \(e=1,2\) survive. At \(e=2\), one has \(j=2,m=1\)
and \(E_*=K\), so the quadratic setup and all subsequent deductions
in file 78 are valid directly. Its two final exclusions therefore
apply. \(\square\)

## 5. Scope

Theorem 81.1 replaces a supposed quadratic core with an actual finite
space of square-root choices. The size bound is independent of (M).
For cubic orbits, it reduces the new field freedom to four possibilities.
Theorem 81.3 handles the entire four-choice branch by one parameterized
genus inequality. The eight-choice branch and the large reduced degrees
remain open; this note does not prove that the explicit curves lack a
common finite etale cover.
