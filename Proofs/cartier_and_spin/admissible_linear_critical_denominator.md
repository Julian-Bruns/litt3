# Proof: the remaining critical root is a ratio of quadratic moments

Use the exact
[critical quadratic identity and fifteen-auxiliary model](../../Theorems/cartier_and_spin/admissible_critical_quadratic_incidence.md),
[polynomial annihilator exclusion](../../Theorems/cartier_and_spin/admissible_linear_annihilator_exclusion.md),

## Factor comparison without finite leading-coefficient division

In the short frame the exact identity $U^2-DV_2=FQ$ has
\[
Q=v\rho^2-\delta_3\mu_2-\delta_2\mu_1-\delta_1n_0
-(\delta_3\mu_1+\delta_2n_0)T-\delta_3n_0T^2.
\]
The source minimal polynomial is separable, so $F,D$ are coprime.
Since $R\mid U$ and $R\mid D$, the identity implies $R\mid Q$.
The case $Q=0$ is impossible: squarefreeness of $D$ would give
$D\mid U$, hence a polynomial annihilator of degree at most two,
excluded in every actual degree-ten profile. Thus
$Q=\gamma R\ne0$.

Synthetic division gives
\[
R=T^2+(\delta_2/\delta_3+c)T
+\delta_1/\delta_3+c\delta_2/\delta_3+c^2.
\]
Comparison of the quadratic and linear terms gives
$\gamma=-\delta_3n_0\ne0$ and $\mu_1=cn_0$.
The constant comparison then gives
$v\rho^2=\delta_3(\mu_2-c^2n_0)$.
These are identities over $K$. Their finite integral consequences
will use the equivalent denominator-free equality
\[
Q=-n_0\,D/(T-c).
\]
No division by a finite value of $\delta_3$ is involved in that
equality.

## Infinity gaps exclude several rational-root branches

The short moment bound is $\operatorname{pole}_O\mu_1\le12$.
Since $n_0$ is a nonzero affine function, its pole order is a
nonnegative nongap. If $c$ has pole $r\ge7$, then
$\mu_1=cn_0$ forces $n_0\in L_{12-r}\subset L_5$.
But $L_5=\langle1,x\rangle$, and the two independent high-gap
constraints of the fifteen-auxiliary model kill this space. This is
a contradiction. If $r>12$, the pole bound itself already fails.

For pole four, the same product identity gives
$n_0\in L_8=\langle1,x,x^2\rangle$. In the exact translation
formula
\[
\mu_1=\eta_1-y^2\operatorname{rem}(Zp,P)/P,
\qquad \eta_1\in L_{12},
\]
the two gap constraints kill remainder coefficients $x^9,x^8$.
The third coefficient $x^7$ cannot also vanish for a nonzero
quadratic $p$: the retained three-row matrix has determinant
$[23]\ne0$. Therefore the remainder term has exact pole eleven.
Meanwhile $\mu_1=cn_0$ has pole at most ten. Its affine part
$\eta_1$ must first have pole at most eleven, since a pole-twelve
term could not cancel the remainder. The semigroup of $X$ then
makes that affine part have pole at most ten, which cannot cancel
the exact pole-eleven remainder. This excludes pole four.

## Finite root integrality and the two smallest critical profiles

In the finite frame $w=W+a$, $a=Z/y$, the raw polynomial has
affine coefficients and primitive content at every finite DVR.
The monic polynomial $\phi_f$ also has affine coefficients; division
of $F_f'$ by $\phi_f$ therefore gives an affine critical polynomial
$D_f$. Its cubic coefficient remains $\delta_3$ under translation,
and its root corresponding to $c$ is $c_f=c+a$.

At a finite point let $\ell=\operatorname{ord}\delta_3\ge0$.
If $c_f$ has pole $r>\ell$, the cubic term in $D_f(c_f)$ has
valuation $\ell-3r$. Every lower term has valuation at least
$-2r$, strictly greater than $\ell-3r$. That unique lowest term
cannot cancel. Thus every finite pole of $c_f$ has order at most
$\ell$, and $\delta_3c_f$ is affine regular. This argument allows
all lower-coefficient drops and repeated finite critical fibers.
It does not make $c_f$, or $c$, affine.

Write $f=\delta_3c_f$. Then
$\delta_3c=f-a\delta_3$. For the $m=3$ and $m=6$ profiles,
$\delta_3$ is a polynomial of degree at most one and two,
respectively. The pole bound on $c$ gives
$\operatorname{pole}_O(\delta_3c)\le m+6$.
The non-affine remainder of $a\delta_3$ has possible leading
gap poles seventeen, fourteen and eleven. Descending leading
orders force every gap above $m+6$ to vanish: initially the
affine part has pole at most seventeen and hence at most sixteen,
so the gap-seventeen coefficient is zero; the next step bounds
it by fourteen and hence thirteen, killing the gap-fourteen
coefficient. If the product bound is at most nine, the next
step kills gap eleven as well. This uses leading orders only;
it does not assert that affine functions have no subleading
Laurent coefficients at gaps.

For $m=3$, the first two gap rows are invertible on
$\langle1,x\rangle$, contradicting $\delta_3\ne0$. For
$m=6$, they force $\delta_3$ onto their unique quadratic kernel
line, generated by $q=([13],[18],[24])$. If $c$ has pole at most
three, the third gap also vanishes, contradicting the fixed
three-row determinant $[23]\ne0$. Pole four was already excluded.
Thus $c$ has pole five or six.

Now $\mu_1=cn_0$ and its pole bound twelve give respectively
$n_0\in L_7$ or $L_6$, both equal to
$\langle1,x,x^2\rangle$. The same two gap constraints in the
quadratic-moment model put $n_0$ onto the same line as
$\delta_3$. Both are nonzero, so $\delta_3=k n_0$ for a
nonzero constant $k$. Substitution in the earlier Hankel identity
gives $n_0\mu_2-\mu_1^2=(v/k)\rho^2$.

The exact coprimality of $q$ and $A$ is checked below. At every
selected endpoint, $n_0$ and $\delta_3$ are therefore units.
Finite root integrality makes $c$ regular there. The next section
then shows that $c(P)=0$ must have $\delta_1(P)=0$; no
ordinary-small endpoint survives in this profile.

## The pole-six profile is entirely impossible

The simplified Hankel equality has left terms of pole at most
twenty and exactly $2(6+r)$, respectively, whereas its right
side $(v/k)\rho^2$ has pole at most twenty-two. Pole six for
$c$ would give the unique pole twenty-four and is impossible.
Pole five gives a unique left pole twenty-two, so $\rho$ must
have exact pole six, or polynomial degree two.

Write $U=R p$ with $R$ monic quadratic. The numerator has
degree at most three and its cubic coefficient is
$p_3=U_5=v\rho$, of exact pole sixteen. On each of the five
large sheets, $W$ has exact pole two and $u$ pole ten. Since
$c$ has pole five, the denominator $\delta_3(W-c)$ has exact
pole eleven. Thus $p(W)$ has exact pole twenty-one.

If any coefficient had
$\operatorname{pole}p_j+2j>21$, its nonzero initial polynomial
of degree at most three would vanish at all leading large-sheet
residues. At least four of these residues are distinct by the
actual profile theorem, which is impossible for a cubic. Hence
$\operatorname{pole}p_j\le21-2j$, giving
$\operatorname{pole}p_3\le15$, contrary to sixteen. This
excludes pole five and the entire $m=6$ stratum. No opposite
map, reciprocal trace or finite fiber simplicity is needed.

## A critical-value inequality in all five pole-two profiles

Retain $d=10$, and let $r>2$ be the remaining root's pole.
The two fixed gap rows force the nonzero affine first moment
to have pole $N\in\{6,9,10\}$, and $\mu_1=cn_0$ gives
$N+r\le12$. Write $U=R p$, $k=\deg p\le3$. Since $c$
dominates all short source roots, $F(c)=v\prod_i(c-W_i)$
has exact pole $10+10r$. Canceling $R$ in the critical identity
and evaluating at $c$ gives
\[
\gamma F(c)=R(c)p(c)^2,\qquad\gamma=-\delta_3n_0.
\]
The left side has exact pole $m+N+10+10r$.

On all five large sheets $p(W)$ has pole $10+m+r$.
The same four-residue initial-polynomial argument gives
\[
\operatorname{pole}p_j\le10+m+r-2j,
\qquad\operatorname{pole}p(c)\le10+m+r+k(r-2).
\]
The source coefficient budgets give
$\operatorname{pole}\delta_1\le16$ and
$\operatorname{pole}\delta_0\le18$: writing
$F=\sum F_jT^j$, the identity $F'=\phi D$ gives
$\delta_1=2F_7$ and $\delta_0=F_6$, and the corresponding
products involve three and four roots, each of pole at most two.
The root equation $D(c)=0$ implies
\[
\operatorname{pole}\delta_2
\le\max(m+r,16-r,18-2r),
\]
since a larger pole would make its term uniquely largest.
Consequently synthetic division gives
\[
R(c)=3c^2+2(\delta_2/\delta_3)c+\delta_1/\delta_3,
\qquad\operatorname{pole}R(c)\le B_*.
\]
Comparison of both sides of the exact critical-value identity
now yields precisely the stated inequality.

When $\rho=0$, $k\le2$. For $r=5,6$ the moment bound makes
$N=6$, and $B_*=2r$ in the remaining $m=9,10,12$ profiles.
The required inequality has positive left side, so these root
poles are excluded. For $m=9,r=3$, $B_*=7$. If $N=9$ or
$k\le1$, it again has positive left side. The sole equality
case is $N=6,k=2$. Here $p_2=U_4=v\operatorname{Tr}_hu$,
because $\rho=0$. Its budget is eighteen, but the affine
function $\operatorname{Tr}_hu\in L_{10}$ has nongap pole;
therefore $p_2$ has pole at most sixteen. The other budgets
give $\operatorname{pole}p(c)\le23$, and the critical-value
identity would compare exact pole fifty-five with a right side
of pole at most $7+2\cdot23=53$. This is impossible. There
is no assumption that $\operatorname{Tr}_hu$ is nonzero unless
$k=2$ itself forces its nonzero coefficient.

## Ordinary-small finite branches force zeros of the first moment

At a selected endpoint $P$ the short raw polynomial and critical
polynomial have integral coefficients; the short translation is
regular because $P$ is not a cubic branch point. The exact contact
bound gives $Q(0)(P)=0$. Suppose $c$ is regular with $c(P)=0$.
The polynomial $D/(T-c)$ has integral coefficients by synthetic
division. Its constant term is
$\delta_1+c\delta_2+c^2\delta_3$. Consequently
\[
0=Q(0)(P)=-n_0(P)\delta_1(P).
\]
If $\delta_1(P)$ is a unit, then $n_0(P)=0$. This reasoning
retains zeros of $\delta_3$ and all primitive-content zeros.

The excluded boundary cannot be silently included. When
$\delta_1(P)=0$, this equality permits $n_0(P)\ne0$. In fact
$D(c)=0$ with $c\in rR$ then gives $\operatorname{ord}\delta_0
\ge2$, so $D$ has weight at least two for $r,T$ both of weight
one. Since $F'=\phi D$ and $\phi$ has weight three, the degree-five
initial selected-root polynomial has zero derivative. In
characteristic five all its first slopes coincide. Thus this is
the concentrated boundary, not an ordinary-small branch. Higher
contact cuts remain available, but no universal first-moment zero
is asserted there.

If $\gamma\ne0$, the equation $p(x)+\gamma y=0$ has at most
one solution among the three distinct nonzero $y$ values on any
selected unramified cubic fiber. There are three such fibers, so
at most three selected zeros.

If $\gamma=0$, a nonzero polynomial $p$ of degree at most three
could vanish at all three selected $x$ values only if it were a
nonzero scalar multiple of $A/(x-r)$ for the fourth, unselected
root $r$. The following exact fixed certificate excludes this.
Therefore it vanishes on at most two selected fibers, giving at
most six selected zeros.

The fixed rows are ascending codes. Use
\[

\quad Z=(15,19,24,12,10,19,3,24,18,16).
\]

\[
L_9=(16,5,14,10),\qquad L_8=(18,16,2,16).
\]
Substitute the symbolic quotient $p=A/(x-r)$, valid when $A(r)=0$.
The resulting cubic forms in $r$ are
\[
\ell_9=(17,23,14,11),\qquad \ell_8=(11,4,13,7).
\]
Already the first has the exact Bezout identity
\[
A(r)([4]+[1]r+[21]r^2)
\; +\;\ell_9(r)([15]+[16]r+[22]r^2+[11]r^3)=1.
\]
Thus $A(r)=0$ and $L_9(A/(x-r))=0$ cannot hold simultaneously.
All displayed coefficient arithmetic is in the fixed code field.
